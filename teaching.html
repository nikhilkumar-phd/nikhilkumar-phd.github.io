Teaching

Philosophy and experience

A student understands a concept when they can predict how a real system will behave, recognise when a result is physically impossible, and trust a measurement over a guess.

Physical intuition before the mathematics

The courses I learned best started with a physical picture; the ones I struggled with started with a derivation. So I teach in physical order. A topic opens with a real system and a question about its behaviour such as a rail that wears unevenly through a curve, a shaft that fails at a stress raiser, a vehicle that responds late to a steering input. Then the model, then the mathematics, and then the moment worth teaching toward, where the mathematics predicts something intuition alone could not. Intuition isn't a shortcut around rigour; it's what rigour is meant to sharpen.

Theory meeting measurement

Engineering judgement develops when a clean prediction meets a real measurement. My own background is in building the systems that take those measurements — servo-driven test platforms, sensors, data acquisition — and I prefer teaching towards the collision between the two. A student who has confidently derived a second-order response meets a real sensor with a real time constant, finds the prediction off, and has the hardware as the reason. Those are the hours that turn coursework into judgement. Such projects are always the ones with true meanings.

For that reason I'm specifically interested in teaching laboratory and instrumentation courses, not only lecture courses, and I would build a hardware component into every elective I develop.

Assessment that matches the objective

I favour open-ended assignments and design projects where they fit: model a system, test it against cases the student wasn't handed, defend the result in writing or a short demonstration. What gets graded is not only whether the work runs, but the quality of the assumptions, the validation, and the ability to explain the tradeoffs.

I hold that preference without holding it rigidly. A required, ABET-accredited undergraduate course needs individually attributable evidence that each student met the outcome, so there I keep a meaningful individual examination component and let projects carry the depth. In graduate electives and capstone, the project-centred model applies fully. What a course is certifying should decide how it is assessed.

Experience

I have taught in multiple settings at Virginia Tech and VIT. In Solid Mechanics, a course of roughly fifty students, I ran the problem sessions — working through concepts and connecting them to the case at hand. In Vehicle System Dynamics, with a cohort of around 130, I graded course assessments and held office hours. Across both, the pattern was consistent: students gained real command of the material once they had to connect it to a physical case or a measurement rather than a clean textbook problem.

Mentoring

Mentoring started early for me. In my final undergraduate year I worked with several first- and second-year students, mostly on the distance between a course problem and a real one — how to bound it, what to assume, and when an assumption stops being safe.

During my PhD I mentored undergraduate interns and master's students in the lab, working with them on design problems and on getting data acquisition setups running: selecting sensors, wiring and verifying them and deciding whether a signal can be trusted before it becomes a result. That last step is the one students skip most often, and it is where I spend the most time with them. What I want them to leave with is the habit of interrogating their own measurement first.

I would gladly advise a student design team and recruit undergraduate researchers from one. A student who has spent a season fighting a real vehicle problem walks into a course already asking the questions I would otherwise have to invent.
