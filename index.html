About

The longer version

Trained in mechanical engineering, working at the point where contact mechanics, tribology, and vehicle dynamics meet — and where the answers have to be measured, not assumed.

What I do

Hi, I'm Nikhil. My work sits at the interface where a mechanical component carries its highest load, and my job is to find out what actually happens there. Usually that means building something that can measure it. Across transportation and machinery, the parts under the highest load are the ones that wear, fatigue, and fail, and the cost of missing that failure is measured in lives rather than downtime.

Building the systems that measure

A lot of my work is hardware. In my postdoctoral role I was part of the expansion of a single-wheel contact rig into a single-axle test platform — specifying servo motors and drives, integrating new motion axes into an existing control architecture, building the safety circuits, and calibrating custom instrumentation. Before that, my doctoral results depended on test protocols I designed from scratch, repeatable enough that a federal sponsor could act on the numbers.

Design of experiments, instrumentation, data acquisition, and enough mechanics to know what the signal means: that combination is the thread through everything below, and it transfers well beyond the specific components I have tested.

Before research

I studied Mechanical Engineering at the Vellore Institute of Technology, graduating in 2022, and spent my last two years there as an undergraduate research assistant doing finite element contact modelling of stepped and back-to-back bolted joints in ANSYS APDL — parametric evaluation of stress and strain at frictional interfaces. That work won Best Paper at a peer-reviewed conference and is where the interest in contact started.

My senior project took me into manufacturing: a systematic process improvement in tooling on the 3C machining lines at Toyota Engines India, working inside the Toyota Production System. Seeing how a part is actually made changed how I think about failure. A component's service life is set as much by its material, its surface, and its process as by the load it eventually carries.

Doctoral research

I completed my PhD in Mechanical Engineering at Virginia Tech in 2025, in the Railway Technologies Laboratory within the Center for Vehicle Systems and Safety, advised by Dr. Mehdi Ahmadian. The work examined how surface condition and contact geometry change the forces at a loaded interface: how contaminants and engineered friction modifiers alter available traction, and what the contact patch actually looks like when the geometry stops being Hertzian.

Two results stand out. I produced one of the first experimental characterisations of non-Hertzian conformal and two-point contact using a real wheel profile, measured directly rather than inferred. And I developed a wear optimisation that minimised modelled wear rate using sequential quadratic programming on a modified Archard law, validated against baseline testing. The programme was delivered to the Federal Railroad Administration and produced five peer-reviewed journal articles.

Where the work is going

Three connected directions. First, the contact mechanics and tribology of load-bearing interfaces, including the material, surface, and manufacturing choices that govern wear — the same mechanics that governs a wheel on a rail governs bearings, gears, and brakes. Second, non-destructive detection of defects before they cause failure, extending passive acoustic sensing to a broader class of components. Third, predictive maintenance: combining sensor data with physics-based models to time intervention before failure rather than after it.

Long term, I want to run an experimentally grounded lab built around those three questions, with problems scoped so that undergraduate and master's researchers can own one from bench to result.

Outside the lab

Outside the lab I play the violin, follow cricket more closely than my calendar can really justify, and take almost any excuse to travel — ideally with a trek attached to it. Music and long walks are where I do my least structured thinking, which is probably why that's where most of the useful ideas show up. I hit the gym almost 7 days a week!

Track record

2022 — present

Test infrastructure specified and commissioned, experiments designed and run, and results delivered to a federal sponsor on schedule — most of it built on measurements that did not exist before the hardware was there to take them.

  • 01 Designed and built electromechanical test infrastructure, expanding roller rig capability from single-wheel to single-axle testing. Specified servo motors and drives and integrated three new motion axes into an existing control architecture, bridging hardware to control model.
  • 02 Developed and implemented lab safety protocols — STO, E-Stop, and RTO — to meet operational safety standards on a high-energy test platform.
  • 03 Stress-tested and calibrated a custom PCB for wheel instrumentation, validating signal integrity under operational loads before any measurement was trusted.
  • 04 Applied JMP-based design of experiments across multiple degrees of freedom, producing statistically rigorous technical reports for federal stakeholders rather than one-off runs.
  • 05 First experimental characterisation of non-Hertzian conformal and two-point contact using an AAR2A wheel profile — measured directly with pressure-sensitive films rather than inferred.
  • 06 Achieved a 95% reduction in modelled wear rate through constrained optimisation on a modified Archard law solved by sequential quadratic programming, validated against baseline testing on the rig.
  • 07 Quantified how contaminants change available traction at a loaded contact, covering both traction-reducing and traction-enhancing agents. Findings delivered to the Federal Railroad Administration.
  • 08 Six journal articles, three conference papers, and six invited talks and posters since 2020, plus peer review for SAE, the International Journal of Rail Transportation, and Railway Engineering Science.
